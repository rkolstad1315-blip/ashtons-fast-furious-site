A high energy dog needs more exercise than most owners have time to give on a weekday, and the right amount depends on the dog. The [American Kennel Club](https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/how-much-exercise-does-dog-need/) puts it plainly: the answer varies from dog to dog and depends on age, health, and breed. For a working breed that was bred to herd, guard, or pull all day, a lap around the block is a warm up, not a workout.

This post is for the owner of a Malinois, a German Shepherd, a Husky, or any dog that is wrecking the house by Wednesday. It covers how to tell your dog is under exercised, why walks alone often fall short, what counts besides running, and where a supervised treadmill session fits.

## The short answer

There is no single number that fits every dog, and we will not invent one. Three things decide it:

- **Breed and job.** Herding and working breeds were selected for stamina. They settle after real effort, not after a stroll.
- **Age and health.** Puppies and seniors need shorter, gentler sessions, and a dog with joint or heart issues needs a vet's guidance before anything strenuous.
- **The individual dog.** Two Shepherds from the same litter can have different needs.

A practical test: after exercise, does your dog settle on their own within a few minutes, or are they still pacing and looking for something to do? A dog that settles got enough. A dog that does not is telling you something.

## Signs your dog is under exercised

Working breeds rarely suffer in silence. The usual signs:

- **Destructive chewing**, especially of things that smell like you: shoes, remotes, the corner of the couch.
- **Barking at everything**, including sounds that never used to matter.
- **Pacing or patrolling** the house or fence line without a reason.
- **Not settling** in the evening, even after dinner and a walk.
- **Pulling and lunging** on the leash as if the walk is the only outlet of the day.
- **Digging** craters in the yard.

None of these are stubbornness. They are a dog with a full tank and nowhere to pour it. Training helps with the manners, but the fuel has to go somewhere first.

## Why walks alone often are not enough

A leash walk at a human pace is a slow trot for a large dog. It is good for sniffing, bathroom breaks, and bonding, and it should stay in the routine. It is not an aerobic workout for a dog built to cover miles.

## Mental work counts too

Physical exercise is only half of it. The AKC notes that mental stimulation is not a replacement for physical exercise, but it is an important complement. For a working breed, a tired mind settles a body faster than you would expect.

Cheap options that take ten minutes:

- **Sniff walks.** Let the dog choose the route and the pace for part of the walk. Nose work is tiring.
- **Puzzle feeders and scatter feeding.** Dinner takes fifteen minutes instead of fifteen seconds.
- **Short training sessions.** Five minutes of new behaviors, twice a day, beats one long session.
- **Find it games.** Hide treats or a toy and let the dog hunt.

Pair mental work with real physical effort and most high energy dogs become easier to live with within a couple of weeks.

## Where a treadmill session fits

A supervised treadmill session gives a dog steady, focused exercise with no traffic, no other dogs, and no heat. It supplements walks rather than replacing them. The walk is still where your dog sniffs, sees the neighborhood, and spends time with you. The treadmill is where the extra energy goes on the days a walk cannot do the job.
