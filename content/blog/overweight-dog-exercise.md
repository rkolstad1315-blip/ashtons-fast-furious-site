If your vet has said your dog needs to lose weight, the safe order is: agree on a target with the vet, fix the food, then build exercise slowly from where your dog is today, not from where you wish they were. Exercise matters, but for weight loss it is the supporting act, and pushing a heavy dog too hard too soon is how joints and hearts get hurt.

This post covers the vet conversation, why food comes first, low impact ways to build fitness, how to track progress at home, and how we adjust sessions for heavier and older dogs.

## Start with the vet

Before changing anything, get a weight, a target, and any limits from your vet. The [American Veterinary Medical Association's](https://ebusiness.avma.org/files/productdownloads/mcm-client-brochures-pet-healthy-weight-2023.pdf) client guide on healthy weight says the first step is an honest conversation with your veterinarian about your pet's weight. Ask specifically:

- What is a healthy target weight for this dog?
- Are there joint, heart, or breathing limits on how hard they can work?
- Is there an underlying condition, such as a thyroid problem, behind the weight gain?
- How often should we weigh in?

The AVMA guide lists the payoff: a healthy weight lowers the risk of arthritis, diabetes, high blood pressure, breathing problems, back problems, kidney disease, and some cancers. That is worth a slow, careful plan.

## Food matters more than exercise

Most of a dog's weight loss happens in the bowl. A dog cannot out run a diet of free fed kibble and table scraps, and trying to burn it off with hard exercise is risky for a dog already carrying extra load.

Work with your vet on portions and a measured feeding schedule. The AVMA guide suggests non food rewards, such as walks, play, and praise, in place of treats, and notes that they can be just as rewarding. If treats stay, count them in the daily total.

Exercise still earns its place. It protects muscle while the weight comes off, improves mood and sleep, and gives a bored dog something to do besides think about food.

## Low impact ways to build up

The goal is steady effort without pounding. Good options:

- **Short, frequent walks.** Several short walks a day beat one long one for a heavy dog. Build minutes slowly, week over week.
- **Swimming**, where available. Buoyancy takes the load off joints.
- **Controlled treadmill work.** A treadmill gives a steady, even pace with no hills, no uneven ground, and no hot pavement, and a handler can stop the moment the dog shows strain.
- **Scent games and training.** Mental work burns energy without impact.

In Jacksonville, NC, heat limits summer walks for every dog, and a heavy dog overheats sooner. Keep outdoor work to early morning and after sunset, and check the pavement with the back of your hand first.

## Track progress at home

Weigh in once a month, at the same vet or the same scale, and write it down. Between weigh ins, use a body condition score. The [World Small Animal Veterinary Association's body condition chart](https://vmc.vet.osu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/wsava-bcs-chart-dogs-cats.pdf) is a nine point scale, with the middle scores as ideal. The [American Animal Hospital Association](https://www.aaha.org/resources/is-my-pet-overweight/) describes the ideal body condition as one where you can feel the ribs without a layer of fat over them and can see a waist and an abdominal tuck.

Score is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. Let the vet confirm it, and adjust food and exercise based on the trend, not a single number.

## How sessions adjust for heavier and older dogs

A supervised treadmill session gives an overweight dog focused, low impact exercise inside a climate controlled unit in your own driveway, with a person at their side every minute. Your dog wears a harness and is never tied by the neck, and no dog is ever forced to keep running. Water and rest breaks are part of every session.

## Where to start this month

1. Book the vet visit and leave with a target weight and any limits.
2. Measure every meal and swap treats for play or praise.
3. Add a few minutes to the daily walks each week, and try one controlled session.

Check with your vet first before starting any exercise plan for an overweight dog.
